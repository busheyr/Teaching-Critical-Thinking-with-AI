Session Overview

This session examines how clinical instruction operates when learners have the ability to generate answers quickly using AI tools. The emphasis is on how these tools shape thinking within clinical tasks, rather than on limiting their use. Instruction remains focused on how decisions are made under realistic conditions.

Clinical tasks are structured around patient scenarios with incomplete information and multiple possible interpretations. Faculty should expect learners to determine what information is relevant, weigh competing options, and justify a chosen course of action. The priority is the quality of reasoning demonstrated, not the speed or completeness of the response.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, faculty should be able to:

  • Evaluate how learners identify and prioritize relevant clinical information
  • Assess how learners compare competing interpretations before selecting an action
  • Judge the clarity and specificity of reasoning used to support clinical decisions

What Critical Thinking Looks Like in Clinical Work

Clinical work requires decisions that lead to action. A learner must decide what to do using the information available in that moment. That information is often incomplete, and it may point in more than one direction. The quality of thinking is judged by how the learner handles that uncertainty.

A response that only describes findings does not show thinking. A response that selects an action and explains why it fits the situation does.

Core Demands

Relevance

The learner determines which details affect the decision. This requires filtering out information that does not change the plan of care. For example, a learner may receive a full patient history but must isolate the findings that influence the immediate clinical question.

Interpretation

The learner considers more than one explanation for the same set of findings. Each interpretation must be weighed against the available data. This prevents premature closure, where a single explanation is accepted without comparison.

Action

The learner selects a course of action based on the strongest interpretation. The decision must connect directly to the information identified as relevant. The learner must also explain why this action is more appropriate than other options.

These demands do not occur in sequence. A learner may adjust what counts as relevant while comparing interpretations or revise a decision as new data is considered. This requires continuous control over how information is used, not a fixed step-by-step approach.

What Weak Thinking Looks Like

Some responses appear complete but avoid decision-making. These responses often:

  • List findings without linking them to a decision
  • Present one interpretation without considering alternatives
  • Select an action without explaining why it fits the case

These patterns suggest that information is recognized but not used.

What Strong Thinking Looks Like

Stronger responses show how information leads to a decision. This typically includes:

  • Prioritizing findings that directly affect the clinical question
  • Comparing at least two plausible interpretations
  • Selecting one action and explaining why it is justified

The explanation must connect the data, the interpretation, and the decision.

Clinical thinking is demonstrated when a learner makes a decision and supports it with clear reasoning tied to the case.

How AI Can Shape Student Thinking

Tools that generate responses can assist with early stages of a task. They can organize large amounts of information, outline standard approaches, and model how ideas connect within a clinical context. When used deliberately, they can help learners see how a response might be structured.

These tools also change how learners begin a task. Instead of forming an initial position, a learner may start by reviewing a complete response. This shifts the role of the learner from generating reasoning to evaluating or selecting from what is already produced.

How AI Can Shape Student Thinking

Tools that generate responses can assist with the early stages of clinical tasks. They can organize information, model common approaches, and show how different elements of a case might be connected. This can be useful when a learner is unfamiliar with a task or needs support structuring a response.

At the same time, these tools can change how a learner engages with the task itself. Instead of determining what matters in the case, the learner may begin by reviewing a completed response. This shifts the task from constructing reasoning to evaluating what has already been produced. When this becomes the default approach, fewer decisions are made by the learner during the process.

Where the Shift Occurs

Initial Position

A learner may delay forming an interpretation of the case. Instead of asking “What do I think is happening?” the learner asks “What does the tool produce?” This reduces the need to identify relevant findings before seeing an answer.

Use of Information

Information may be accepted as presented rather than selected and applied. A response can include accurate details without showing how those details influenced a decision.

Comparison of Options

Generated responses often present one dominant interpretation. Without deliberate effort, the learner may not consider alternative explanations or test competing options against the case.

Decision Commitment

When multiple reasonable actions exist, the learner may adopt the one provided without examining why it is stronger than other possibilities.

These changes do not indicate that the learner lacks knowledge. They indicate that fewer decisions are required during the task. The structure of the tool reduces the need to choose, compare, and justify.

Common Response Patterns

Some patterns appear consistently when learners rely on generated responses without modifying their approach:

  • The response moves directly to a conclusion without showing how the conclusion was reached
  • Key findings are included but not prioritized or connected to the decision
  • Only one interpretation is presented, even when the case supports more than one
  • The selected action is appropriate but not clearly justified
  • Language is precise, but reasoning is not visible

These responses often appear complete because they are well organized. However, they do not show how the learner worked through the problem.

Why Responses Appear Stronger Than They Are

Generated responses often include features that signal quality:

  • Clear structure with labeled sections
  • Correct terminology used in the appropriate context
  • Logical flow between points

These features can make a response seem more developed than it is. The structure can mask missing steps in reasoning. A response may look complete while still avoiding key decisions.

Instructional Impact

When these patterns are present, it becomes difficult to evaluate how a learner is thinking. The instructor sees a finished product but cannot determine how the learner interpreted the case or why a decision was made.

This leads to several instructional challenges:

  • The reasoning process is not visible in the submitted work
  • Different learners may produce nearly identical responses
  • Errors in judgment are harder to detect because the structure appears correct
  • Feedback becomes less specific because the point of breakdown is unclear

Instruction must therefore make the decision process visible. Tasks must require learners to state what they considered, what they rejected, and why they selected a particular course of action.

Implications for Task Design

If a task allows a complete response to be submitted without showing reasoning, the learner can rely on the structure provided by the tool. To counter this, tasks must require decisions that cannot be bypassed.

Effective adjustments include:

  • Requiring learners to identify which findings influenced their decision
  • Asking for comparison between at least two plausible interpretations
  • Requiring justification for why one action was selected over others
  • Designing prompts where different choices lead to different outcomes

These changes force the learner to engage with the case rather than accept a generated response.

Design Implementation Ideas

Fixed Action Menu

A fixed action menu limits the response to a defined set of choices. The learner cannot respond in general terms. They must select one option and proceed from that choice. This changes the task from describing what could be done to deciding what will be done.

This approach is most effective when the options are all reasonable. If one option is clearly correct, the task becomes recall. If each option has tradeoffs, the learner must examine the case closely before choosing.

How It Works in Practice

The instructor presents a short clinical scenario followed by a small set of actions. The learner selects one and explains why it fits the case better than the others.

A basic structure:

  • A focused patient scenario with relevant details
  • Three or four possible actions
  • A requirement to select one option
  • A short explanation tied to the case

The explanation must refer to specific findings. General statements are not sufficient.

Why This Changes the Task

Forces Commitment

The learner must choose one path. This removes the option to describe multiple approaches without deciding.

Reveals Differences

When options are placed side by side, small differences become important. The learner must determine which differences matter for this case.

Limits Evasion

A fixed menu prevents broad or unfocused responses. The learner cannot rely on general clinical language to avoid making a decision.

What Becomes Visible

When learners work within a fixed set of actions, specific aspects of thinking can be evaluated:

  • Whether the selected option aligns with key findings
  • Whether the learner understands the consequences of each choice
  • Whether the explanation connects the case details to the decision

Errors are easier to identify because the decision point is clear.

Design Considerations

Option Quality

Each option should be plausible. Include choices that reflect common errors or incomplete reasoning, not just incorrect answers.

Case Alignment

Details in the scenario should support more than one option at first glance. This requires the learner to weigh evidence rather than match patterns.

Explanation Requirement

Require a brief justification. Without it, the task only shows selection, not reasoning.

Attach Consequences to Each Option

A decision should lead to a result. If nothing follows the choice, the task ends too early. The learner selects an option but never sees what that option produces. This limits what can be learned from the task.

When consequences are built in, the learner must deal with the outcome of the decision. The result may confirm the choice, reveal a problem, or introduce a new issue that must be managed. This extends the task beyond selection into evaluation.

What the Instructor Sets Up

The instructor prepares outcomes in advance for each option in the menu. Each outcome should reflect what would reasonably happen if that action were taken in practice.

A simple structure:

  • A clinical scenario with a clear decision point
  • Three or four possible actions
  • A defined outcome attached to each action
  • A follow-up prompt that asks the learner to respond to the result

The outcome should be specific. It should change the situation in a way the learner must address.

What the Learner Does

After selecting an option, the learner is shown the result of that choice. They must then interpret what happened and decide what to do next.

This often involves:

  • Identifying whether the outcome improved or worsened the situation
  • Explaining why the outcome occurred based on the original decision
  • Adjusting the plan if the result introduces new risks or complications

The learner is not only judged on the initial choice, but on how they respond to what follows.

How This Changes Evaluation

Evaluation shifts from checking whether the answer is correct to examining how the learner handles the outcome. A correct decision with weak reasoning can still be identified. An incorrect decision can still produce useful work if the learner recognizes the issue and responds appropriately.

This makes it possible to see:

  • Whether the learner understands the link between action and result
  • Whether they can recognize when a decision leads to a problem
  • Whether they can adjust their approach based on new information
Patterns That Emerge

When consequences are used consistently, patterns in judgment become easier to track across tasks. Some learners may repeatedly choose options that create avoidable complications. Others may recognize issues quickly but struggle to adjust their plan.

These patterns provide specific points for feedback. Instead of general comments, the instructor can refer to how the learner responded to particular outcomes and where their reasoning needs to change.

Force Tradeoffs

Some clinical choices are difficult because each option solves one problem while creating another. One action may reduce immediate risk but delay needed treatment. Another may move treatment forward but increase the chance of harm. When a task includes this kind of tension, the learner cannot rely on a simple best answer. They must decide which concern should carry more weight in that situation.

This changes the work of the task. The learner is not searching for the one option with no downside. They are comparing costs, limits, and likely effects. The quality of the response depends on whether the learner can explain why one drawback is more acceptable than another.

What the Instructor Builds Into the Task

To force a tradeoff, the instructor designs the options so that none of them is fully comfortable. Each option should offer a benefit, but each should also create a meaningful cost.

This can be done in several ways:

One option supports speed but reduces certainty

One option protects safety but delays action

One option addresses the main problem but creates strain elsewhere

One option gathers more information but uses time the patient may not have

The tradeoff must be real. If one option is clearly safer, faster, and more appropriate than all others, the learner is not prioritizing. They are simply spotting the best answer.

What the Learner Is Actually Doing

The learner reads the case and identifies the competing concerns built into it. Then the learner must decide which concern takes priority and why.

That means the learner is doing several concrete things:

Identifying the conflict

The learner names the two or three concerns that are in tension. These might involve urgency, safety, comfort, uncertainty, available resources, or likely outcomes.

Judging the cost of each option

The learner looks at what each action gives and what it gives up. This requires more than describing the options. It requires judging the practical effect of each cost.

Choosing what matters most

The learner decides which risk, delay, or limitation is most acceptable in that case. This is the center of the task.

Explaining the priority

The learner states why one concern should come first under those conditions. The explanation must be tied to the facts of the case, not to a general rule stated in isolation.

Why This Matters

A tradeoff changes the kind of thinking the task requires. The learner cannot succeed by recalling a standard response and applying it automatically. They must decide how to act when important goals cannot all be achieved at once.

This matters because clinical work often requires that kind of judgment. A clinician may need to protect stability before pursuing completeness. In another case, acting quickly may matter more than gathering every detail first. The task should make the learner confront that tension directly.

It also prevents a common weakness in classroom responses. Learners often write as if every good clinical decision achieves all goals at the same time. A tradeoff removes that illusion. It makes the learner account for what is lost as well as what is gained.

What Good Task Design Looks Like

A strong tradeoff task is built around a specific point of tension. The learner should be able to see that there is a decision to make, but not resolve it immediately without thought.

Useful design features include:

Plausible options

Every option should be defensible at first glance. The learner should need to think before ruling one out.

Relevant costs

The cost attached to each option should matter clinically. Minor inconveniences do not create a meaningful tradeoff.

Case details that affect priority

The facts of the case should influence which cost is more acceptable. The learner should need to use those details to decide.

A required explanation

The prompt should ask the learner to explain not only what was chosen, but what was accepted or sacrificed by making that choice.

What Becomes Visible in Student Work

When tradeoffs are built into a task, the instructor can see more than whether the learner selected a defensible option. The response shows how the learner sets priorities.

This makes several things easier to observe:

  • Whether the learner can recognize competing concerns in the case
  • Whether the learner understands the practical cost of each option
  • Whether the learner can defend one priority over another
  • Whether the explanation is tied to the case rather than to vague principles

This kind of task also reveals a difference between weak and strong justification. A weak response often treats one option as obviously right and ignores what it costs. A stronger response acknowledges the cost, explains why it is acceptable here, and shows awareness of what is being given up.

A Simple Example of the Structure

An instructor presents a patient scenario with a decision that involves timing, risk, and incomplete information. The learner receives three possible actions.

  • One action moves quickly but relies on limited information
  • One action gathers more certainty but delays intervention
  • One action reduces immediate risk but does not address the larger problem

The learner must choose one. Then the learner explains what priority drove the decision and what cost was accepted by making it.

That explanation is where the thinking becomes visible. The instructor can see whether the learner understood the tension in the case or simply chose the option that sounded safest on paper.

Sequential Decisions

Many clinical tasks are presented as if one answer completes the work. A learner reads a case, chooses an action, and stops. That structure hides an important part of clinical thinking because real decisions do not end the situation. One choice changes the conditions of the case, and the next choice must be made from there.

A sequential task is built around that reality. The learner makes an initial decision, receives new information, and then must decide again. The next step is shaped by what was done earlier. This makes the task less about producing a single strong response and more about managing a developing situation.

What the Instructor Builds

The instructor designs the case in stages rather than all at once. The learner does not receive the full scenario upfront. Instead, the case unfolds across a short sequence.

A basic structure might look like this:

Step 1: Initial presentation

The learner receives the first set of findings and must decide what to do first.

Step 2: New development

The instructor introduces a change. This might be a new symptom, a response to treatment, a test result, or a complication.

Step 3: Follow-up choice

The learner must decide how to respond now that the situation has changed.

Step 4: Brief reflection

The learner explains how the earlier decision affected the next step.

This structure makes the task cumulative. The learner cannot treat each moment as separate from the one before it.

What the Learner Is Actually Doing

In a sequential task, the learner is not simply answering three different prompts. The learner is carrying a line of judgment forward over time.

That means the learner must:

  • make an initial choice using limited information
  • notice how the case changes after that choice
  • decide whether the original plan still fits the situation
  • continue, revise, or reverse the plan based on what now matters most

This is different from answering isolated questions. The learner must keep track of the logic of the case from one step to the next.

Why This Matters

A single-response task can make reasoning look stronger than it is. A learner may choose a plausible action once, but that does not show whether the learner can manage a case as it develops. Sequential decisions test whether thinking holds together over time.

This matters because later decisions often depend on how the early problem was framed. If the learner misunderstood the first step, that misunderstanding may affect everything that follows. A staged task makes that chain visible.

It also changes the kind of attention the learner must give to the case. Instead of focusing only on the immediate question, the learner must consider how the current decision may shape later options.

What Strong Performance Looks Like

Strong work in a sequential task is not just a series of correct answers. It shows that the learner understands the relation between one step and the next.

That often includes:

A clear starting judgment

The first decision is tied to the facts available at the time, not to information that appears later.

Adjustment without drift

When new information appears, the learner updates the plan for a reason. The revision is connected to the change in the case.

Continuity in reasoning

The learner does not treat each step as unrelated. The explanation shows awareness of how earlier choices shaped the current position.

Recognition of error

If the first decision created a problem, the learner can identify that problem and respond to it directly rather than ignoring it.

What Weak Performance Looks Like

Sequential tasks also expose weaknesses that are harder to see in one-step assignments.

Common problems include:

  • making an initial choice and then repeating it even when the case changes
  • changing direction without explaining why the new information matters
  • treating each stage as a separate question with no connection to the earlier decision
  • failing to recognize that an earlier choice limited later options

These responses suggest that the learner is reacting to each prompt rather than managing a continuous clinical situation.

How to Design the Sequence Well

A useful sequence should be short enough to stay focused but long enough to show development. In most cases, two or three decision points are enough.

To make the task work, the instructor should ensure that:

The next step should require thought, not just confirm the first answer.

The learner should be able to see that the first action affected what became possible or necessary afterward.

Each added detail should force the learner to reconsider, refine, or defend the plan.

The learner should explain how the reasoning developed across the sequence.

Where This Helps Most

Sequential decisions are especially useful when the goal is to examine how learners respond to change. They work well in cases where timing matters, patient status evolves, or early interpretation influences later action.

They are also useful when an instructor wants to see whether a learner can recover from a weak initial choice. In a single-response task, that opportunity never appears. In a staged task, the learner has to deal with the consequences of earlier judgment and decide what to do next.

A Simple Example

An instructor presents an initial patient scenario and asks the learner to choose the first action. After that choice, the instructor reveals that the patient’s condition has changed or that a new result has come back. The learner must then decide whether to continue, adjust, or abandon the original plan.

At the end, the learner explains two things: why the first decision made sense at the time, and why the second decision did or did not require a change in direction.

That explanation shows whether the learner can sustain reasoning across time rather than produce one isolated answer.

What This Means for Faculty Practice

Improving thinking does not require adding new lectures, readings, or cases. The change occurs in how existing material is used. A case that once asked for description can be rewritten to require a decision. A discussion prompt that asked for explanation can be revised to require a choice and a defense.

This keeps the content stable while changing what learners are required to do with it. The instructor’s role shifts from presenting information to shaping how learners act on that information during the task.

Where Instruction Changes

Case Use

Instead of asking learners to walk through a case, the instructor places a decision point inside it. The learner must act at that point rather than continue describing the situation.

Timing of Information

Information can be released in parts instead of all at once. This forces the learner to act before the full picture is available and adjust as new details appear.

Response Expectations

The assignment specifies what the learner must produce. A complete response includes a selected action and a short explanation tied to the case. General summaries are no longer accepted as full answers.

How to Modify an Existing Assignment

Most assignments can be revised without changing the topic or learning materials. The instructor edits the prompt, not the content.

A practical sequence:

  • Identify where a decision could occur in the current case or question
  • Rewrite the prompt so the learner must choose one action
  • Add one competing option that could reasonably be selected
  • Require a short explanation that connects the decision to specific details

These changes can be made in a single assignment without redesigning the entire course.

What Faculty Should Look For in Student Work

When tasks require decisions, evaluation shifts to what the learner does with the case. The instructor reviews how the learner uses information, not how much is included.

Focus on:

  • Whether the selected action matches the details of the case
  • Whether the explanation refers to specific findings rather than general statements
  • Whether the learner distinguishes between options when more than one is plausible

This allows feedback to target how the learner is thinking, not just whether the answer aligns with expectations.

Managing the Change in Practice

These adjustments can be introduced gradually. Faculty do not need to redesign every assignment at once. A single revised task can show how students respond to the new structure.

It is also useful to compare responses before and after the change. In many cases, the amount of writing decreases while the clarity of the decision increases. This makes grading more direct because the point of evaluation is clear.

Starting Point

Begin with one assignment that already uses a case or scenario. Apply a small set of changes:

  • Insert a clear decision point
  • Provide two or three reasonable options
  • Require a brief justification tied to the case
  • Review how clearly the learner connects the decision to the evidence

After reviewing the results, the same approach can be applied to other parts of the course.
